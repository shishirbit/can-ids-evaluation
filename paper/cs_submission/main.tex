\title{What do CAN intrusion detection benchmarks measure?\\
Evaluation traps, cross-vehicle failure, and a pair of\\ identifier-agnostic detectors}

\author[a]{First Author}
\ead{author@univ.edu}
\author[a]{Second Author}
\author[a]{Third Author}
\affiliation[a]{organization={Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University XXXX},
                city={City}, country={Country}}

\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning intrusion detection for the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus is routinely
reported at above 99\% accuracy, yet almost nothing is known about how those detectors behave at a
usable false-alarm rate or on a vehicle they were not trained on. We re-examine the question with
an event-level evaluation protocol: capture-level cross-validation, whole held-out normal drives
for calibration, $k$-of-$n$ alarm smoothing, an alarm duty-cycle cap, and detection reported at a
stated false-alarm budget. Four findings follow. First, the most widely used benchmark encodes its
own answer: in the HCRL Car-Hacking dataset every attack is separated by a gap of exactly
\SI{3.0}{\second} (coefficient of variation 0.001--0.003), so a logistic model with a single
feature -- time since the last attack -- reaches a pre-onset AUC of 1.000 where a spatio-temporal
graph network reaches 0.851, and window-level detection is saturated (AUC 1.000). Second, on the
ROAD dataset a 98\,k-parameter convolutional--recurrent network matches a
1.05\,M-parameter graph model ($p=0.81$ over five seeds, identical detected-event sets) and
both stop at the same 27-of-29 ceiling, so architectural complexity does not pay. Third, five
evaluation choices -- threshold-grid resolution, seed count, an alarm duty cap during threshold
selection and again on reported metrics, and whether held-out normal driving comes from different
drives -- each individually reverse the apparent ranking of these models, so any one of them alone
suffices to publish a spurious improvement. Fourth, on can-train-and-test these detectors do not
transfer between vehicles at all (0 of 44 events across four vehicle pairs) and no amount of
unlabelled target-vehicle traffic repairs it, whereas representations that describe each frame
relative to its own arbitration identifier recover 69--78\% of those events with no labels from the
target vehicle. Running two such identifier-agnostic detectors with OR-ed alarms detects 81\% of
unknown-vehicle events at \SI{0.3}{\per\hour} false alarms using two 98\,k-parameter models
(\SI{0.20}{\mega\byte} int8, $\approx$40\% of one CPU core). Code, splits and per-event results are
released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Controller Area Network (CAN) carries the safety-critical traffic of essentially every
production vehicle and provides neither authentication nor encryption, so intrusion detection has
become the pragmatic defence \citep{rajapaksha2022survey}. The literature reports detection
accuracies above 99\% on public benchmarks with striking consistency
\citep{song2020dcnn,lo2022hydlids,aljabri2025lightweight}. Two questions that decide whether such a
detector could be deployed are, however, rarely asked: how many false alarms does it raise per hour
of ordinary driving, and does it work on a vehicle other than the one it was trained on?

This paper asks both. We keep the models deliberately ordinary -- a convolutional--recurrent
network over raw frames, a graph-attention network over arbitration identifiers, gradient-boosted
trees, and two unsupervised detectors -- and concentrate on the evaluation. Every result is reported
at the event level: an alarm is a rising edge of a $k$-of-$n$ rule, a false alarm is such an edge on
attack-free traffic, and detection is reported at a stated false-alarm budget with a cap on the
fraction of time the alarm is active.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An event-level evaluation protocol for CAN IDS} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}):
capture-level cross-validation in which an attack and its masquerade variant never separate, whole
held-out normal drives for threshold calibration, $k$-of-$n$ alarm smoothing, an alarm duty-cycle
cap, and detection reported against a false-alarm budget rather than at a window-F1 optimum.

\item \textbf{Evidence that the standard benchmark measures the injection schedule}
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}): Car-Hacking's attacks are separated by gaps of exactly
\SI{3.0}{\second}, a single-feature timer beats a spatio-temporal graph network at every forecast
horizon, and window-level detection is saturated. We also document two artefacts of the dataset
that affect anyone using it.

\item \textbf{Five evaluation traps} (Section~\ref{sec:traps}), each demonstrated on one codebase
and each sufficient on its own to reverse the ranking of the models we compare.

\item \textbf{A cross-vehicle failure and a fix} (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}): identifier-embedding
detectors transfer to nothing (0/44 events on four vehicle pairs), unlabelled adaptation traffic
does not help, identifier-agnostic representations recover most of the gap, and two such
representations run as separate detectors with OR-ed alarms are complementary and cheap
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Datasets.} The HCRL Car-Hacking corpus \citep{song2020dcnn} is the most used CAN IDS
benchmark. \citet{verma2024road} introduce ROAD, argue that public datasets contain attacks that are
``too easily detectable'', and provide physically verified fabrication, fuzzing and masquerade
attacks on one vehicle. \citet{lampe2023cantt} release can-train-and-test, whose four vehicles from
two manufacturers support explicit known/unknown vehicle splits, and \citet{kidmose2025cansleuth}
show that this dataset exposes vehicle overfitting that the HCRL corpora cannot.
\citet{rajapaksha2024canmirgu} contribute real moving-vehicle attacks including masquerade and
suspension.

\paragraph{Detectors.} Deep detectors dominate: convolutional networks over identifier images
\citep{song2020dcnn}, recurrence plots \citep{desta2022reccnn}, hybrid convolutional--recurrent
stacks \citep{lo2022hydlids}, multimodal time-interval and signal models \citep{kang2024canival},
and $n$-gram methods designed for microcontroller budgets \citep{stabili2022daga}. Uncertainty is
commonly obtained through Monte-Carlo dropout \citep{gal2016dropout}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Concerns about benchmark-driven results are established in network
intrusion detection generally \citep{flood2024baddesign,goldschmidt2025datasets} and increasingly in
the automotive case: \citet{sharmin2024benchmarking} propose an evaluation design space, and
\citet{koltai2026cantrust} build a cross-dataset CAN IDS benchmark and find that reported
performance depends strongly on dataset idiosyncrasies. Our protocol is complementary and more
operational: it fixes an alarm rule and a false-alarm budget, which is what a vehicle integrator
must specify.

\paragraph{Transferable representations.} Closest to our Section~\ref{sec:transfer} are two recent
works. \citet{khreasat2026relational} stress-test statistical, structural-transition and
graph-topology features across attacks and platforms and report a hierarchy of what transfers,
finding that payload-semantic attacks remain invisible to transition-based features.
\citet{hegde2026residualization} propose per-identifier behavioural residualisation -- window
statistics z-scored against each identifier's own baseline -- and argue that the representation
rather than the detector drives performance. Neither evaluates cross-\emph{vehicle} transfer under a
false-alarm budget. We re-implement the residualisation representation, show that it transfers
across four vehicle pairs, and show that it is complementary to the per-frame relative
representation we propose.

\section{Data and preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Car-Hacking \citep{song2020dcnn}.} Four attack captures (DoS, fuzzy, gear and RPM
spoofing) plus attack-free traffic. Parsing the raw logs exposes two artefacts. First, every attack
file contains exactly 300 injection episodes whose inter-episode gaps are \SI{3.0}{\second} with a
coefficient of variation of 0.001--0.003 (Fig.~\ref{fig:carhacking}a). Second, the same
\SI{481.6}{\second} attack-free recording (891{,}068 byte-identical frames) is appended to all four
attack files, and \texttt{Fuzzy\_dataset.csv} additionally contains two recording sessions separated
by a \SI{2540}{\second} silence. We de-duplicate the appended recording and split logs at recording
gaps; without this, a chronological split places every attack in the training portion.

\paragraph{ROAD \citep{verma2024road}.} We use the nine dynamometer ambient captures
(\SI{72}{\minute} of normal driving) and all attack captures with a labelled injection interval: 15
physically verified injection events, 14 of which also have a masquerade variant, giving 29 attack
events. Frame labels are reconstructed from the published metadata (interval, target identifier,
byte pattern); as a check, in non-masquerade captures the target identifier carries exactly twice as
many frames inside the interval as there are injected frames, and in the masquerade variants the two
counts coincide. Sparse injections (the coolant attack sends 43 frames over \SI{4}{\second}) are
closed into a single episode with a \SI{1}{\second} gap-filling rule so that one injection counts as
one event.

\paragraph{can-train-and-test \citep{lampe2023cantt}.} We use the four \texttt{set\_01}--\texttt{set\_04}
sub-datasets, each defining a known and an unknown vehicle (Impala$\rightarrow$Silverado,
Traverse$\rightarrow$Forester, Silverado$\rightarrow$Forester, Forester$\rightarrow$Traverse; three of
the four pairs cross manufacturers), with per-frame attack labels.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Traffic is aggregated into \SI{100}{\milli\second} windows. A detector produces one score per
window; the alarm rule fires when at least $k$ of the last $n$ windows exceed a threshold. A
\emph{false alarm} is a rising edge of that rule on attack-free traffic, expressed per hour; an
attack \emph{event} is detected if the rule fires inside its episode, and latency is measured from
episode onset. Four further rules make the numbers comparable:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Capture-level cross-validation.} On ROAD, fold $f$ tests attack instance $f{+}1$,
validates on the next and trains on the rest; an attack and its masquerade variant always share a
split, because they are the same physical event.
\item \textbf{Whole held-out normal drives.} Calibration and test normal traffic come from
different recordings, never from time slices of the same drive.
\item \textbf{Alarm duty-cycle cap.} The fraction of attack-free time in alarm must not exceed 2\%,
both when selecting the threshold and in the reported result.
\item \textbf{Report against a budget.} We report events detected at $\leq$5, 10, 30 and
\SI{60}{\per\hour} false alarms, sweeping the threshold, rather than at the window-F1 optimum.
\end{enumerate}

Sanity baselines must be beaten before any claim is made: \emph{persistence} (the current window's
own label), a \emph{timing} model using only time since the last attack and time since the recording
started, per-identifier \emph{rate exceedance}, per-(identifier, byte) \emph{payload novelty}, and a
gradient-boosted tree over all identifiers' window features.

\section{What the standard benchmarks measure}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\subsection{Car-Hacking rewards learning the schedule}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/carhacking_schedule.pdf}
\caption{Car-Hacking. (a) Every attack file contains 300 episodes separated by gaps of exactly
\SI{3.0}{\second}. (b) On pre-onset windows, a logistic model whose only feature is the time since
the last attack outperforms a spatio-temporal graph network at every forecast horizon.}
\label{fig:carhacking}
\end{figure}

Because the schedule is fixed, ``forecasting'' an attack reduces to counting seconds. On the fuzzy
capture, evaluated only on windows in which no attack is yet in progress, a one-feature timer
reaches AUC 1.000, 0.996 and 0.988 at horizons of 1, 2 and \SI{5}{\second}, against 0.851, 0.877 and
0.847 for a graph-attention network with Monte-Carlo dropout (Fig.~\ref{fig:carhacking}b). Pooling
all four attack files the timer still leads at \SI{1}{\second} (0.980 versus 0.901). Window-level
detection on the same data is saturated: F1 0.9997 at AUC 1.000, matching published results and
leaving no headroom to demonstrate a contribution.

\subsection{A detectability hierarchy}

Attacks fall into three tiers, and the tier -- not the model -- decides what evidence is required.
\emph{Rate} attacks change an identifier's frame rate. \emph{Value} attacks write byte values that
identifier never normally carries; a per-(identifier, byte) novelty detector finds 17 of 17 of them.
\emph{Contextual} attacks write a perfectly ordinary value that is wrong only in the light of other
identifiers -- ROAD's reverse-light attacks set the light state while the vehicle is not in reverse
-- and the same novelty detector finds 0 of 12. Cross-identifier evidence is therefore necessary for
the third tier, but it need not be a graph neural network: a gradient-boosted tree over all
identifiers' features in the current window finds 12 of 12.

\section{Five evaluation traps}
\label{sec:traps}

Each of the following, applied alone to the same code and data, reverses the ranking of the models
in Table~\ref{tab:road}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Threshold-grid resolution.} Searching 60 candidate thresholds made payload-novelty
features appear to add $+4.2$ events ($p=0.029$); with 200 candidates the same comparison gives
$p=1.000$ and identical event sets.
\item \textbf{Seed count.} With three seeds the graph model appeared to add 11 events over its
ablation; with five seeds the difference is $p=0.81$. Single-seed results at strict budgets are
uninterpretable here: the same configuration scored 7, 27, 24, 27 and 25 events across seeds.
\item \textbf{Duty cap during threshold selection.} A threshold low enough that the alarm never
clears produces exactly one rising edge per recording. Without the cap this scores as ``29/29
events at \SI{29}{\per\hour}'' -- a detector that is always firing.
\item \textbf{Duty cap on reported metrics.} The same trap reappears at reporting time: on an unseen
vehicle our detector reports 8/8 events at \SI{21.9}{\per\hour} while its alarm is active 99.9\% of
the time (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
\item \textbf{Composition of held-out normal traffic.} Calibrating on time slices of the same drives
used for testing made thresholds appear to transfer; a budget of \SI{60}{\per\hour} set that way
produced 1244--\SI{2866}{\per\hour} on unseen drives. Holding out whole drives brings the same budget
to \SI{29}{\per\hour}.
\end{enumerate}

A further trap concerns latency. The alarm rule, not the model, sets the floor: a $k$-of-$n$ rule
cannot fire before $k$ windows. Reporting a raw single-window threshold gives a median latency of
\SI{0}{\milli\second} (detection inside the onset window), but the stable 3-of-5 configuration costs
\SI{220}{\milli\second}; the unsmoothed alarm that meets a sub-\SI{100}{\milli\second} target has no
feasible threshold for two of five seeds (Table~\ref{tab:latency}).

\section{Cross-vehicle transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}

\subsection{Identifier-embedding detectors transfer to nothing}

Trained on one vehicle of a can-train-and-test pair and evaluated on the other, the detector detects
0 of 44 events across the four pairs at any valid operating point (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross}). The raw
numbers look deceptively good -- with the source vehicle's threshold it flags 8/8 events on the
target -- but its alarm is active 99.9\% of the time, and once the threshold must satisfy a
false-alarm budget on the target vehicle's own attack-free traffic, detection falls to zero. Removing
the payload features changes nothing, so this is not a feature artefact: the learned identifier
embeddings and traffic patterns are vehicle-specific, and the target vehicle has 98 arbitration
identifiers against the source's 52.

Supplying unlabelled traffic from the target vehicle does not repair it. Adding its identifiers to
the vocabulary and 1, 5 or \SI{15}{\minute} of its attack-free frames as negatives, with the
threshold calibrated on a held-out fifth of that traffic, yields 0--1 of 8 events at a valid
operating point with no trend in the amount of traffic. The mechanism is visible: those identifiers'
embeddings are trained exclusively on normal traffic, so the classifier has never seen an attack on
any of them.

\subsection{Identifier-agnostic representations transfer}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figs/cross_vehicle.pdf}
\caption{Unknown-vehicle detection at $\leq$\SI{5}{\per\hour} false alarms, thresholds calibrated on
the target vehicle's own attack-free traffic (no target labels), three seeds. Identifier-embedding
detectors score zero on every pair.}
\label{fig:cross}
\end{figure}

We describe each frame only \emph{relative to its own arbitration identifier}: cadence ratio against
that identifier's warm-up median inter-arrival time, payload bit- and byte-change rates, excursion
beyond that identifier's warm-up byte range, per-identifier and bus-wide rate ratios, and DLC change
-- twelve features, no identifier embedding and no cross-vehicle vocabulary. Per-identifier
references come from a \SI{30}{\second} warm-up prefix of each capture, so adapting to a new vehicle
requires neither labels nor retraining. We compare against our re-implementation of per-identifier
behavioural residualisation \citep{hegde2026residualization}, which z-scores 14 window statistics
against each identifier's baseline.

Both transfer. Pooled over the four pairs and three seeds, the relative representation detects
34.3/44 unknown-vehicle events (78\%) and residualisation 30.3/44 (69\%), against 0/44 for the
identifier-embedding detector. The finding is therefore a property of identifier-agnostic
representations in general, not of one feature set. They differ in their blind spots: on masquerade
attacks residualisation reaches 40\% against 18\% for the relative features.

Concatenating the two feature sets into a single 26-feature input \emph{fails}: unknown-vehicle
detection drops to 53\% at \SI{5}{\per\hour} and 60\% at \SI{30}{\per\hour}. On one pair the combined
model collapses to 0/10 because its scores on the target vehicle's clean traffic saturate near 1.0,
so no threshold meets the budget -- with the source vehicle's threshold it still finds 10/10, i.e.
discrimination survives while calibration fails.

\subsection{Two detectors, OR-ed alarms}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figs/pair_union.pdf}
\caption{Two identifier-agnostic detectors with OR-ed alarms, each given half the false-alarm
budget, on the unknown vehicle and on masquerade attacks (four pairs $\times$ two seeds).}
\label{fig:pair}
\end{figure}

Because the blind spots are disjoint, we run both representations as separate detectors and raise an
alarm when either fires, giving each half the budget. Pooled over four pairs and two seeds
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pair}), the union detects 71 of 88 unknown-vehicle events (81\%) at
\SI{0.3}{\per\hour} false alarms, against 67\% and 69\% for the branches alone at 1.2--1.3, and 83\%
at a \SI{30}{\per\hour} budget. On masquerade attacks the union reaches 38\% at \SI{5}{\per\hour} and
52\% at \SI{30}{\per\hour}, roughly doubling the relative representation alone. The union therefore
improves detection \emph{and} lowers the false-alarm rate, because each branch runs at a stricter
threshold. Masquerade coverage of 38--52\% remains the principal open problem, consistent with the
contextual tier being the hardest case throughout this study.

\section{Deployment cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports batch-1 inference on a single CPU thread. The
98\,k-parameter detector needs \SI{0.10}{\mega\byte} in int8 and about 20\% of one core at
the rate its sliding window implies; the paired design doubles that. Feature extraction costs 0.3\%
of a core over real traffic. By contrast, the 50-pass Monte-Carlo-dropout uncertainty specified in
much of the forecasting literature \citep{gal2016dropout} would require roughly 20 cores at the same
window rate, which rules it out for an embedded deployment. A laptop core is several times faster
than a typical automotive core, so these are optimistic bounds; the duty column is what scales.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

ROAD provides 15 injection events on one vehicle, and can-train-and-test four vehicles from two
manufacturers; our error bars are over seeds, not over vehicles or fleets. Latency is quantised to
the \SI{100}{\milli\second} window. The identifier-agnostic pipeline excludes windows inside its
\SI{30}{\second} warm-up, so attacks beginning in the first \SI{30}{\second} of a capture are not
evaluated and event denominators differ slightly between representations. Suppress attacks, in which
frames are removed rather than injected, are not covered. Finally, both our relative representation
and our residualisation baseline are re-implementations evaluated under one protocol; the original
authors' code may behave differently.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The question that decides deployability of a CAN intrusion detector is not benchmark accuracy but
how many events it catches per hour of false alarms, and whether it survives a change of vehicle. On
the standard benchmark, a one-feature timer matches a graph network because the benchmark's attacks
run on a timetable; on a realistic corpus, a 98\,k-parameter model matches a
1.05\,M-parameter one and both stop at the same data-imposed ceiling; and across vehicles,
identifier-embedding detectors detect nothing at a usable false-alarm rate. What does transfer is a
representation that never names an identifier, and two such representations, run as separate
detectors with OR-ed alarms and calibrated on a new vehicle's own unlabelled traffic, detect 81\% of
that vehicle's attack events at \SI{0.3}{\per\hour} false alarms for \SI{0.20}{\mega\byte} of
parameters. We suggest that future CAN IDS results be reported at a stated false-alarm budget, with
an alarm duty cap, on held-out drives, and -- wherever the data permit -- on a vehicle the detector
has never seen.
