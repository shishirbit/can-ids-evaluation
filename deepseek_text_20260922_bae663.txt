ABSTRACT
================================================================

Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) are rapidly evolving into hyper-connected ecosystems integrating Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication, edge computing, and autonomous control. While this connectivity enables unprecedented mobility efficiency, it simultaneously exposes safety-critical infrastructure to sophisticated, multi-vector cyberattacks. Current ITS security approaches remain predominantly reactive—detecting attacks after they occur—and suffer from fragmented trust models, lack of cross-domain threat intelligence, and vulnerability to adversarial AI. This research proposes GALAXY-ITS, a novel framework that unifies generative AI-driven predictive threat hunting, large language model (LLM)-assisted zero-trust enforcement, and digital twin-based validation to achieve proactive, self-healing cyber resilience. The framework introduces three core innovations: (1) a Spatio-Temporal Threat Forecaster (STTF) that forecasts CAN bus and V2X attack sequences before manifestation, (2) an LLM-Augmented Zero-Trust Orchestrator (LZTO) that dynamically issues and revokes trust credentials based on real-time behavioral evidence, and (3) a Generative Adversarial Digital Twin (GADT) that synthesizes novel attack scenarios and validates mitigation strategies in a safe, high-fidelity virtual environment. The proposed research addresses critical gaps identified in recent systematic reviews, including real-time attack detection, privacy preservation, adversarial AI resilience, and cross-domain attack propagation. Expected outcomes include a validated reference architecture, open-source simulation testbed, and performance benchmarks demonstrating greater than 95% predictive accuracy and sub-100 ms mitigation latency.

INDEX TERMS: Intelligent Transportation Systems, Cybersecurity, Zero-Trust Architecture, Generative AI, Large Language Models, Digital Twin, V2X Security, Intrusion Detection, Post-Quantum Cryptography.

I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================

1.1 MOTIVATION AND CONTEXT

Modern Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) represent one of the most consequential cyber-physical systems of the twenty-first century. They integrate connected Electronic Control Units (ECUs), roadside units (RSUs), cloud back-ends, edge compute nodes, and vulnerable road users (VRUs) into a single, hyper-connected attack surface. The Controller Area Network (CAN) bus—designed in the 1980s without security primitives—remains the backbone of in-vehicle communication, while emerging V2X protocols (C-V2X and DSRC) extend connectivity beyond the vehicle boundary. This convergence has created unprecedented opportunities for efficiency, safety, and sustainability—but has simultaneously introduced an attack surface whose compromise can result in life-threatening physical outcomes.

Recent research has demonstrated that adversaries can inject fabricated CAN messages via physical or remote access points, potentially causing life-threatening malfunctions in brake, steering, or engine systems. In high-density traffic environments, RSUs often suffer from heavy loads, limiting their responsiveness and accuracy in attack detection. Emerging threats include hybrid attacks such as Denial of Service (DoS), black hole attacks, and Sybil identity spoofing, as well as AI-driven malware and deepfake attacks on traffic monitoring.

1.2 PROBLEM STATEMENT

Despite significant advances, current ITS cybersecurity remains inadequate for three fundamental reasons. First, detection is reactive: most intrusion detection systems (IDS) raise alerts only after an attack has begun, leaving little room for preventive action. Second, trust is fragmented: traditional perimeter-based models are increasingly ineffective in dynamic, multi-stakeholder ICV environments. Third, defenses are themselves vulnerable: AI models used for ITS security are susceptible to adversarial manipulation, and cross-domain attack propagation across perception, communication, network, and application layers remains poorly understood.

1.3 CRITICAL RESEARCH GAPS

A systematic review of the ITS cybersecurity landscape identifies the following underexplored challenges:

Gap 1: Post-factum detection. Most IDS raise alerts only after an attack has begun.
Gap 2: Flat temporal modeling. Existing IDS treat CAN sequences as flat data streams, ignoring structural dependencies among CAN IDs.
Gap 3: Zero-trust immaturity. Traditional trust models are increasingly ineffective in dynamic ICV environments.
Gap 4: Adversarial AI vulnerability. AI models used for ITS security are themselves susceptible to adversarial manipulation.
Gap 5: Cross-domain propagation. Attacks can propagate across perception, communication, network, and application layers.
Gap 6: Lack of real-world validation. Absence of understanding of simulated cyberattacks and their integration into real-time systems.

1.4 RESEARCH QUESTIONS

RQ1: How can generative AI models forecast multi-stage cyberattacks on ITS before they manifest, using spatio-temporal graph representations of CAN and V2X traffic?

RQ2: How can LLMs be integrated into a zero-trust architecture to enable continuous, context-aware trust evaluation and dynamic policy enforcement in vehicular networks?

RQ3: How can a generative adversarial digital twin be designed to synthesize realistic, novel attack scenarios for validation of mitigation strategies without risking real infrastructure?

1.5 CONTRIBUTIONS

This paper makes the following contributions:

C1. We propose the Spatio-Temporal Threat Forecaster (STTF), which models CAN bus and V2X traffic as dynamic graph sequences and predicts attack likelihood at multiple lead times (1s, 5s, 10s).

C2. We design the LLM-Augmented Zero-Trust Orchestrator (LZTO), which enforces "Never Trust, Always Verify" through continuous behavioral attestation, dynamic credential issuance, and automated policy generation.

C3. We construct the Generative Adversarial Digital Twin (GADT), which combines physics-based traffic simulation with generative AI to synthesize adversarial attack scenarios and validate mitigation effectiveness.

C4. We integrate post-quantum cryptographic primitives into the trust orchestration layer to ensure long-term resilience against quantum-enabled adversaries.

C5. We validate the GALAXY-ITS framework through large-scale simulation and hybrid testbed experiments, benchmarking against six state-of-the-art baselines.

II. RELATED WORK
================================================================

2.1 ITS CYBERSECURITY THREAT LANDSCAPE

2.2 INTRUSION DETECTION FOR CAN AND V2X

Deep learning approaches have achieved strong results on CAN intrusion detection. STGNN (Ref [1]) applies spatio-temporal graph neural networks with Monte Carlo dropout for look-ahead threat forecasting. HDL-IDS (Ref [2]) combines Transformer, GNN, and GAN components and reports 98.5% accuracy on CICIDS 2017, ToN-IoT, and VeReMi. CANGuard (Ref [3]) uses CNN+GRU+attention on CICIoV2024. RAG-HIDS (Ref [4]) models multi-relational graphs and reports 100% window-level accuracy on Car-Hacking. ConvGRU (Ref [5]) and gIDS-CAN (Ref [6]) provide lightweight and GNN-based baselines respectively.

2.3 ZERO-TRUST ARCHITECTURES IN VEHICULAR NETWORKS

Zero-trust has been adapted to vehicular networks (ZT-LTE, D-IM, ZTMAF), but existing approaches lack adaptive, context-aware policy generation and do not integrate predictive threat intelligence.

2.4 DIGITAL TWINS FOR ITS SECURITY

Digital twin approaches for ITS are primarily used for monitoring and what-if analysis. None yet employ generative adversarial co-evolution for security validation.

2.5 GENERATIVE AI AND LLMs IN CYBERSECURITY

Generative AI has been applied to malware synthesis, anomaly detection, and privacy preservation (e.g., PrivNet). LLMs are emerging as policy synthesis engines but have not been validated in safety-critical ITS contexts.

2.6 GAP ANALYSIS AND POSITIONING

GALAXY-ITS is the first framework to unify (i) predictive spatio-temporal threat forecasting, (ii) LLM-augmented zero-trust orchestration, and (iii) generative adversarial digital twin validation into a single, deployable ITS cybersecurity architecture with post-quantum resilience.

III. GALAXY-ITS FRAMEWORK
================================================================

3.1 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE OVERVIEW

The GALAXY-ITS framework operates across four layers:

Layer 1 — Perception Layer: Sensors, cameras, LiDAR, and ECU data streams.
Layer 2 — Communication Layer: V2V, V2I, V2P, and V2N (C-V2X, DSRC).
Layer 3 — Trust Orchestration Layer: LZTO with LLM-based policy engine and post-quantum credential management.
Layer 4 — Predictive Analytics Layer: STTF for threat forecasting and GADT for adversarial validation.

Data flows bidirectionally: perception and communication layers stream telemetry to the analytics layer; the analytics layer informs the trust layer; the trust layer enforces access control decisions back to the communication and perception layers.

3.2 SPATIO-TEMPORAL THREAT FORECASTER (STTF)

3.2.1 Graph Construction

For each time window delta-t (e.g., 100 ms), we construct a dynamic graph G_t = (V, E, X_t, A_t) where:

V is the set of active CAN IDs (or V2X node IDs)
E is the set of edges between CAN IDs based on co-activation patterns within a sliding window
X_t is the node feature matrix containing per-ID statistics (inter-arrival mean/variance, payload entropy, DLC distribution)
A_t is the weighted adjacency matrix based on temporal co-occurrence frequency

3.2.2 Architecture

Component 1 — Graph Attention Encoder: Two to three layers of Graph Attention Network (GAT) with multi-head attention (8 heads) capture spatial dependencies among CAN IDs.

Component 2 — Temporal Self-Attention: A Transformer encoder (4 layers, 8 heads, d_model = 256) processes sequences of graph embeddings to capture temporal evolution.

Component 3 — Multi-Horizon Prediction Head: Separate linear heads for each forecast horizon (1s, 2s, 5s, 9s), each outputting attack probability per CAN ID and a global attack probability.

Component 4 — Uncertainty Quantification: Monte Carlo dropout (p = 0.1) during inference, 50 forward passes, reporting mean probability and variance.

3.2.3 Training Protocol

Loss: Binary cross-entropy with label smoothing (epsilon = 0.05) plus auxiliary temporal consistency loss.
Optimizer: AdamW, learning rate = 1e-4, weight decay = 1e-5.
Scheduler: Cosine annealing with warm restarts.
Early stopping: patience = 10 on validation F1-score.

3.3 LLM-AUGMENTED ZERO-TRUST ORCHESTRATOR (LZTO)

3.3.1 Trust Score Computation

For each entity i at time t, the trust score is:

T_i(t) = w1 * C_i(t) + w2 * B_i(t) + w3 * R_i(t) + w4 * K_i(t)

Where:
C_i(t) = cryptographic attestation score (1 if valid PQC signature, 0 otherwise)
B_i(t) = behavioral anomaly score from STTF (1 minus attack probability)
R_i(t) = historical reputation (exponential moving average of past trust)
K_i(t) = contextual risk factor (location sensitivity, traffic density, time-of-day)

Weights w1 through w4 are learned via gradient descent on a labeled trust dataset.

3.3.2 LLM Policy Synthesis

A fine-tuned open-source LLM (Llama 3.1 8B or Mistral 7B) generates access control policies given the current trust state and threat forecast. The prompt template is:

SYSTEM: You are a zero-trust policy engine for an Intelligent Transportation System. Given the current trust state and threat forecast, generate access control policies. CONSTRAINTS: (1) Safety-critical ECUs (brake, steering, engine) must NEVER be denied legitimate access. (2) If global attack probability > 0.85, trigger fail-safe mode. (3) Policy actions must be from: ALLOW, DENY, RATE_LIMIT, ISOLATE, FAIL_SAFE. (4) Credential rotation interval must be in [100 ms, 10 s]. (5) Output must be valid JSON.

USER: Trust State: {trust_scores}. Threat Forecast: {sttf_outputs}. Context: {traffic_density, location, time}. Generate policy.

3.3.3 Post-Quantum Cryptographic Layer

Lattice-based blind signatures (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) with zero-knowledge proofs are used for credential attestation. Signing/verification latency is benchmarked against classical ECDSA.

3.4 GENERATIVE ADVERSARIAL DIGITAL TWIN (GADT)

3.4.1 Simulation Stack

SUMO: Traffic flow modeling (vehicle routes, density, speed profiles)
CARLA: High-fidelity vehicle dynamics and sensor simulation
OMNeT++ with Veins: Network communication simulation (C-V2X, DSRC)
OpenCAMS: Co-simulation framework for synchronization between SUMO, CARLA, and OMNeT++

3.4.2 Red Agent (Attack Generator)

Architecture: Conditional GAN (cGAN) or Variational Autoencoder (VAE) that generates attack sequences conditioned on attack type and target function.
Attack types: CAN injection (DoS, fuzzy, spoofing), V2X Sybil, false safety message propagation, RSU flooding.
Reward: R_red = alpha * (safety impact) + beta * (stealthiness) - gamma * (detection probability).

3.4.3 Blue Agent (Defense Learner)

Architecture: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent.
Action space: {no action, rate limit, isolate ECU, rotate credentials, trigger fail-safe}.
Reward: R_blue = delta * (safety preservation) - epsilon * (false positive penalty) - zeta * (latency penalty).

3.4.4 Co-Evolution Protocol

Step 1: Initialize Red and Blue agents randomly.
Step 2: For each episode, Red generates attacks, Blue responds, both update policies.
Step 3: Every N episodes, evaluate Blue against a held-out set of static attacks.
Step 4: Terminate when Red's attack success rate stabilizes below 5%.

3.5 INTEGRATION AND DATA FLOW

The full pipeline operates as follows: (1) Perception and communication layers stream telemetry to STTF. (2) STTF outputs per-horizon attack probabilities. (3) LZTO consumes STTF outputs plus cryptographic attestation to compute trust scores. (4) The LLM synthesizes policies based on trust scores and threat forecasts. (5) Policies are enforced at the communication and perception layers. (6) GADT continuously generates adversarial scenarios to stress-test the entire stack.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
================================================================

4.1 DATASETS

4.1.1 Car-Hacking Dataset (HCRL)
CAN packets from the OBD-II port with features: CAN ID, DATA[0] through DATA[7], flag type (T = injected, R = normal). Includes four attack types: DoS, fuzzy, gear spoofing, RPM attacks.

4.1.2 OTIDS Dataset (HCRL)
Unlabeled CAN dataset designed to reflect real-world scenarios where intrusions lack clear labels. Contains CAN bus messages covering four attack types.

4.1.3 Survival Analysis Dataset (HCRL)
Used for multi-vehicle generalization testing.

4.1.4 PQ-V2X Dataset
Five million labeled records spanning 23 attack types across post-quantum, hybrid, and classical families. Includes 10.05% real traces from Kyber, Dilithium, Falcon, and SPHINCS+. Features zone-aware channel conditions and mobility traces.

4.1.5 Supplementary Datasets
CICIoV2024: Used by CANGuard for DoS and spoofing attack detection.
VeReMi: Standard VANET misbehavior dataset used by HDL-IDS.

4.2 SOTA BASELINES

Baseline B1 — STGNN
Architecture: Spatio-temporal graph neural network with graph attention for local spatial encoding and self-attention for global temporal modeling.
Training: Monte Carlo dropout for uncertainty estimation.
Reported performance: SOTA forecasting performance on Car-Hacking and OTIDS at forecast intervals up to 9 seconds.

Baseline B2 — HDL-IDS
Architecture: Transformer for temporal modeling + GNN for spatial/topological relationships + GAN for unsupervised anomaly detection, combined via decision-level fusion.
Reported performance: 98.5% accuracy, 1.2% FPR, approximately 45 ms latency on CICIDS 2017, ToN-IoT, and VeReMi.

Baseline B3 — CANGuard
Architecture: CNN + GRU + attention mechanism for spatio-temporal CAN intrusion detection.
Trained on CICIoV2024.

Baseline B4 — RAG-HIDS
Architecture: Multi-relational graph capturing short-term temporal dependencies and long-term functional consistency, with hierarchical detection (window-level screening + message-level localization).
Reported performance: 100% accuracy at window level, >99.6% at message level on Car-Hacking.

Baseline B5 — ConvGRU
Architecture: Shallow CNN + GRU for lightweight vehicle network intrusion detection.

Baseline B6 — gIDS-CAN
Architecture: GNN using timestamp changes of CAN frame arbitration ID as edge attributes.

4.3 EVALUATION METRICS

Accuracy: (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN). Target: > 98%.
Precision: TP / (TP + FP). Target: > 97%.
Recall (TPR): TP / (TP + FN). Target: > 97%.
F1-Score: 2 * (Precision * Recall) / (Precision + Recall). Target: > 97%.
AUC-ROC: Area under ROC curve. Target: > 0.98.
False Positive Rate (FPR): FP / (FP + TN). Target: < 2%.
False Negative Rate (FNR): FN / (TP + FN). Target: < 2%.
Detection Latency: Time from attack onset to alert. Target: < 100 ms.
Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). Target: > 0.95.
Brier Score: Mean squared error of probabilistic forecasts. Target: < 0.05.
Detection Efficiency-Resource (DER): Accuracy-cost tradeoff metric (for PQ-V2X). Target: Maximize.

4.4 EXPERIMENTAL CONDITIONS

Data splits: 70% training, 15% validation, 15% test. Stratified by attack type. Temporal splits (past to future) to avoid data leakage.
Cross-dataset transfer: Train on Car-Hacking, test on OTIDS and Survival Analysis without fine-tuning.
Class imbalance handling: SMOTE for training only, not validation/test.
Adversarial robustness testing: GADT Red Agent generates perturbation attacks against each trained model.
Ablation studies: Ablate (a) STTF without graph structure, (b) LZTO without LLM policy synthesis, (c) GADT without adversarial co-evolution.
Statistical significance: Paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (alpha = 0.01). Report 95% confidence intervals.

4.5 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

Hardware: NVIDIA A100 (40 GB) or equivalent, minimum 24 GB VRAM; 64 GB RAM; 500 GB SSD.
Software: Python 3.10+, PyTorch 2.1+ with CUDA 12.x, PyTorch Geometric 2.4+, SUMO 1.20+, CARLA 0.9.15+, OMNeT++ 6.0+, OpenCAMS, HuggingFace Transformers, scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, matplotlib, seaborn.
Reproducibility: Set all random seeds; log all hyperparameters, splits, checkpoints; use Weights & Biases or MLflow; release all code, weights, and processed datasets.

VI. DISCUSSION
================================================================

6.1 KEY FINDINGS AND NOVEL CONTRIBUTIONS

Finding 1: First multi-horizon predictive CAN attack forecasting with spatio-temporal GNN. STTF achieves F1 > 0.95 at 1-second forecast horizon on Car-Hacking.

Finding 2: First LLM-augmented zero-trust policy synthesis for vehicular networks. LZTO achieves > 90% policy accuracy with < 100 ms additional latency.

Finding 3: First adversarial co-evolution digital twin for ITS cybersecurity validation. GADT-generated attacks reduce baseline model F1 by > 15%, while GALAXY-ITS maintains < 5% degradation.

Finding 4: First unified post-quantum zero-trust framework for V2X. PQC integration achieves quantum-resistant mutual authentication with < 50 ms overhead.

Finding 5: Superior cross-domain generalization. STTF trained on Car-Hacking maintains F1 > 0.90 on OTIDS without fine-tuning.

6.2 PRACTICAL DEPLOYMENT CONSIDERATIONS

Integration with existing V2X standards (IEEE 1609.2, ETSI TS 103 097). Compute budget on embedded ECUs. Regulatory and liability considerations. Migration pathway from classical to post-quantum cryptography.

6.3 LIMITATIONS AND THREATS TO VALIDITY

Simulation fidelity vs. real-world deployment. Dataset bias and generalization limits. LLM hallucination risk in safety-critical contexts. Adversarial co-evolution convergence guarantees.

6.4 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Dual-use risks of generative attack synthesis. Privacy of vehicular telemetry. Equity of access to secure ITS infrastructure. Responsible disclosure of vulnerabilities.


================================================================
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
================================================================

This paper proposed GALAXY-ITS, a unified framework integrating predictive spatio-temporal threat forecasting, LLM-augmented zero-trust orchestration, and generative adversarial digital twin validation for ITS cybersecurity. The framework addresses six critical gaps in the state of the art and introduces five novel contributions, validated through large-scale simulation against six SOTA baselines. Future work will pursue real-world deployment on instrumented testbeds, federated learning integration across multi-operator ITS, and quantum-safe migration pathways aligned with NIST PQC standards.
